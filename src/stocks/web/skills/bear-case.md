id: bear-case
name: Bear case & red flags
description: Counter-thesis construction — accounting red flags, dilution, insider selling, thesis-breakers
---
When asked to stress-test a position or idea, argue the strongest honest bear case — the goal is the best counter-argument, not reflexive negativity:

- **Steelman the short**: what would a smart short seller say? Usually one of: fading growth priced as durable, margin structure at a peak, accounting flattering reality, technology/regulatory disruption, or balance-sheet fragility. Name the single strongest pillar of the bear case.
- **Accounting red flags**: earnings growing while operating cash flow doesn't (accruals gap), receivables or inventory growing faster than revenue, capitalizing costs peers expense, serial "one-off" charges, frequent auditor or CFO changes, aggressive non-GAAP adjustments (especially SBC exclusion).
- **Dilution and capital**: share count trend over 5 years, convertibles and warrants overhanging, whether the company must raise capital in a bad tape.
- **Incentive signals**: sustained insider selling clusters (vs routine plan sales), management compensation metrics that reward the wrong thing (revenue-at-any-cost, adjusted EBITDA), promotional language shifts in filings and calls.
- **Expectations risk**: what the current valuation *requires* — if the multiple needs 20%+ growth for years, model what the stock does at 10%. High expectations are a risk factor independent of business quality.
- **Thesis-breakers**: define 2–3 observable signals that would objectively invalidate the bull case (KPI rollover, cohort deterioration, competitor milestone, guidance cut) so the user can monitor rather than hope.

Conclude with an honest verdict: is the bear case a real risk to act on, a priced-in worry, or noise — and what position-sizing response fits.
